Winter is not the most popular time to move in New York City, but plenty of people do it every year. Leases end, closings happen, and life doesn't always wait for May. If you're facing a move between November and March, the good news is that it's entirely doable. The bad news is that it requires more planning than a summer move. Here's what to think through.

## Why winter moves are different

The obvious one is weather. Snow, sleet, and freezing rain affect everything from driving to footing on stairs and sidewalks. Trucks have to move more carefully, crews work more slowly, and a day that started dry can turn complicated by mid-morning. NYC's narrow streets and dense parking situations make weather delays worse than they'd be in a suburb.

Beyond weather, shorter days mean less working light. A move that starts at 8 a.m. and runs long will finish in the dark, and that changes the pace of the whole job.

Cold also affects your belongings. Electronics, instruments, wood furniture, and anything with adhesives or finishes don't love freezing temperatures. A piano or a flat-screen TV left in a cold truck for hours can be damaged even without a scratch.

## Choosing a date

Give yourself more scheduling flexibility in winter than you would in summer. A few things to factor in:

**Monitor the forecast closely.** Book your move with the ability to shift a day or two if the weather is going to be brutal. Talk to your mover about their policy on weather delays when you book.

**Avoid school breaks and holidays.** Parking and building access in NYC can get surprisingly complicated around major holidays. The weeks around Thanksgiving, Christmas, and New Year's tend to be chaotic. Mid-January through mid-February is often the quietest stretch.

**Start early in the day.** In winter especially, you want to finish before the temperature drops further in the evening. An 8 a.m. start is better than a noon start.

## Building logistics

[Residential moves](/services/residential) in NYC always involve building coordination, and winter adds a layer. A few things to confirm with your building in advance:

- **Certificate of Insurance (COI).** Your mover will need to file a COI with both buildings before the move. Get the building's COI requirements early so there's no delay. We handle COI paperwork as a standard part of the job.
- **Freight elevator reservations.** Book as far in advance as possible. Time slots fill up, and you don't want a narrow window on a day when everything is running slow.
- **Entry point protection.** Snow and slush tracked through lobbies is a real issue. Good movers bring runners and protect the floors, but confirm with your building what they require.

## Protecting your belongings from the cold

A few items need extra attention in winter:

- **Electronics.** Laptops, TVs, audio equipment, and anything with a screen should be packed in insulated boxes or blanket-wrapped and loaded last so they're not sitting in the cold. Give them time to reach room temperature before powering them on at the new place.
- **Wood furniture and instruments.** Extreme cold can dry out and crack wood. Pianos especially are sensitive. If there's a gap between your move-out and move-in, [temperature-controlled storage](/storage) keeps your items in a stable range, which matters far more than just avoiding freezing.
- **Artwork and antiques.** Same principle. Temperature swings are rougher on fine pieces than moderate cold is. For high-value items, our [specialty handling services](/services/ffe-designer) include the crating and care that fragile pieces need in any season, but particularly in winter.

## What movers do on a winter move

Experienced crews adjust their approach for the conditions:

- Extra runners and floor protection at building entrances and on stairs
- Slower, more careful loading and unloading to account for ice or wet surfaces
- Blanket wrapping on anything sensitive to cold
- Shorter periods with doors open when possible to limit heat loss and cold exposure

Expect the job to take a bit longer than the same move in good weather. That's not inefficiency, it's the right pace for conditions.
