Renovating an apartment in New York City rarely goes as planned. A kitchen gut-renovation that was supposed to take six weeks turns into ten. A bathroom remodel expands when the walls open up. And all the while, your furniture, rugs, art, and everything else you own is sitting in a space that's about to get dusty, loud, and full of contractors.

What you do with your belongings during that window is a decision most people leave too late. Here's what to think through before the first wall comes down.

## Why leaving things in place usually costs you

Construction dust is pervasive. It gets into everything: fine-grit residue from drywall, plaster, or tile work that settles into fabric, coats electronics, and permanently marks upholstered pieces. Even covering furniture with plastic sheeting is imperfect. Plastic traps moisture against finishes, and anything left in an active work zone is at risk from accidents, dropped tools, and foot traffic.

For a partial renovation -- say, just a bathroom or a single room -- you can often consolidate belongings into a clean zone. But for kitchens, living areas, or anything that affects common space, keeping your things in the apartment is often impractical and sometimes damages the very pieces you were trying to protect.

## Think through what actually needs to leave

Not everything has to go into storage. Work with your contractor to map exactly which areas will be active, which will be accessible, and which are genuinely clean zones throughout the project.

Items worth removing:

- Rugs and upholstered furniture near work areas
- Art, mirrors, and wall-hung items in adjacent rooms (vibration travels)
- Electronics and anything sensitive to dust
- Heirlooms, collectibles, or anything you cannot replace

Leave behind what is heavy, rarely touched, or genuinely out of the way -- large built-ins, bookshelves you can seal off, storage furniture in unaffected rooms.

## Short-term storage vs. a longer gap

If your renovation is a few weeks, you need a facility that holds space for short-term contracts without penalizing you for not committing to six months. Some facilities do, some do not -- ask directly about minimum terms before you sign.

If the project runs longer, or you're combining a renovation with a temporary move (staying with family, subletting, an extended hotel stretch), storage timing gets more complex. You may need items loaded in stages, or retrieved mid-project as spaces come back online. A moving company that handles both transport and storage under one roof simplifies that considerably.

## Temperature-controlled storage for the things that matter

Heat and cold cycles are hard on wood furniture, musical instruments, artwork, and electronics over time. If your renovation runs through a New York summer or a cold winter -- both common in a city timeline -- temperature-controlled storage keeps those swings from warping a solid-wood table or stressing a canvas.

## Coordinating the handoff to contractors

Before the movers arrive, walk through the apartment with your contractor and mark what stays and what goes. Clear paths for both the moving crew and the construction team.

Confirm freight elevator reservations if you're in a building that has one. NYC buildings often limit move-out windows to specific times, and if you don't lock in a slot early, you can find yourself competing with another resident's delivery on a tight day.

Also confirm whether your building requires a Certificate of Insurance from your mover. Most co-ops and condos do, with specific coverage minimums and requirements. This needs to be arranged in advance, not the morning of the move. We handle COIs on every [residential move](/services/residential) we do, so it's part of the standard process.

## Planning the return move

The move back in deserves as much care as the move-out. Confirm the renovation is actually complete before scheduling movers. Not "almost done" -- signed off and cleaned. Fresh paint needs time to cure, and new tile grout should be fully set before heavy furniture comes in.

Build a day between contractor hand-off and move-in if you can. It gives you time to clean, verify the work, and do a final walkthrough without movers waiting.

If the project uncovered something unexpected (they often do), that buffer also gives you room to adjust the timeline without paying for a rescheduled truck.
