A lot of the most sought-after apartments in New York City are in buildings without elevators. Pre-war brownstones in the West Village, walk-up tenements in Astoria, rowhouses in Carroll Gardens: the character that makes these places appealing comes with narrow staircases and no freight elevator in sight. That's fine. But a walk-up move is a different job from an elevator move, and knowing what to expect means fewer surprises on move day.

## What makes walk-up moves different

The obvious thing is stairs. For a general move, stairs add time and physical demand. Crews carry everything by hand from the truck to your floor, one load at a time. The higher the floor, the more each trip costs in time and effort.

The specifics matter a lot:

- **Stairwell width.** Many NYC walk-ups were built in the 1800s and early 1900s. Stairwells can be narrow, with sharp turns at each landing. Some furniture (sectionals, California king mattresses, certain wardrobes) simply won't fit the route. A good mover will ask about this before the move, not at the door.
- **Banister clearance.** Low or inward-facing banisters limit how items can be tilted through turns.
- **Floor count.** A third-floor walk-up is manageable. A fifth floor in a building with steep, tight stairs takes considerably more time and crew.

Movers typically factor stair count into the estimate. Ask about it when you get quotes so there are no surprises.

## What to do before the move

A little preparation makes the whole job faster.

**Measure before you commit.** Before signing a lease, measure the stairwell and any turns, then measure your largest furniture pieces. This is especially important for sofas, bed frames, and large dressers. Sometimes a piece won't fit through a prewar stairwell, and it's far better to know that before move day than to find out at the door.

**Declutter aggressively.** Every item that goes up those stairs adds time. A walk-up is a good reason to sell or donate the pieces you were lukewarm about anyway.

**Pack smart.** Boxes going up multiple flights of stairs should be manageable in weight. Ask your mover about their box weight limits, since heavier boxes are slower and harder to carry safely. If you're using [full-service packing](/services/residential), tell the crew about the stairs upfront so they pack accordingly.

**Clear the stairwell.** Let your neighbors know about the move and make sure hallways and stairwells are free of bikes, strollers, and anything else on move day.

## Building rules still apply

Just because there's no freight elevator doesn't mean there are no building requirements. Many NYC walk-up buildings still require a Certificate of Insurance from the moving company before they'll allow the move. Some also restrict move-in hours to protect other tenants.

Check with your building management before confirming a move date. A mover experienced with NYC buildings handles COI requests as a matter of routine. Scanio has been coordinating building paperwork for [residential moves](/services/residential) in New York since 1941.

## Choosing the right mover for a walk-up

Not all moving crews are equally prepared for a walk-up job. A few things to look for:

- A crew size matched to the job. Walk-up moves often benefit from an extra person to manage the stairwell efficiently.
- Experience with NYC prewar buildings, where layouts can surprise you.
- Proper equipment: furniture dollies, stair-climbing tools, and good straps so items are controlled on every step.
- Honest communication about what will and won't fit, before the truck arrives.

If your furniture needs to come apart to fit a tight stairwell, a good mover tells you that in advance and helps you plan for it. Last-minute disassembly on the sidewalk adds time and stress to everyone's day.
