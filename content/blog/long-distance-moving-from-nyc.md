Leaving New York for another city or state is a significant undertaking. The scale is different from a local move, the regulations are different, and the consequences of a bad mover are much harder to walk back once your belongings are on a truck headed somewhere far away. Here's what actually matters when planning a long-distance move out of NYC.

## How long-distance moving is different

A local move in New York is generally priced by the hour. Long-distance moves are priced by weight and miles traveled, which changes the calculation entirely. The more you bring, the more you pay, and there's no hourly clock running to keep things moving. That's why decisions about what to take and what to leave behind matter more on a long-distance job.

The regulatory side is also more involved. Movers handling interstate jobs must be licensed with the US Department of Transportation, not just the state. Scanio holds US DOT 537054 and ICC MC93512 in addition to NY DOT T11495, so we're equipped for both local and long-distance work.

## Getting an accurate estimate

For a long-distance move, there are two main estimate types: binding and non-binding.

A **binding estimate** locks in a price regardless of the final weight. What you're quoted is what you pay, as long as you don't add items.

A **non-binding estimate** is based on the carrier's projection of the weight. The final bill reflects the actual weight, which can come in higher or lower.

Either way, the starting point is an accurate inventory. Walk through every room with your mover, be honest about what you're taking, and don't leave the basement or storage unit out of the conversation. Underestimating the load is the most common reason estimates come in low and final bills come in higher.

## The NYC piece: getting out of the building

Even before the truck heads south on the Jersey Turnpike, there's the matter of getting everything out of a New York building. That part is still a local move in every practical sense.

If you're in a building with freight elevator rules, Certificate of Insurance requirements, or specific move-out hours, those still apply. Scanio handles COIs and elevator coordination on every [residential move](/services/residential), so the building logistics are covered the same way they would be for a local job.

Street parking for a long-haul truck is also worth planning in advance. A 53-foot trailer is not the same as a local moving van, and in dense neighborhoods, that requires a permit or some coordination with the building and neighbors.

## What to do with the things you're not taking

Most people leaving New York aren't taking everything with them. Some items don't make financial sense to ship long-distance, things whose replacement cost is lower than their shipping cost. Others are being held until a new place is ready.

[Storage](/storage) is a practical middle step for both cases. Scanio's temperature-controlled facility in Secaucus, NJ is close to the city and a reasonable option for items you want to keep but don't need on the other end immediately. You pay to store, not to ship, until the timing works.

## The long-distance move timeline

Long-distance jobs don't move on the same timeline as local ones. A few things to know:

- **Pickup and delivery windows.** Unlike a same-day local move, long-distance moves often involve a pickup window (a range of dates) and a separate delivery window. Build flexibility into your plans on the receiving end.
- **Pack ahead.** Full-service packing is available, but on a long-distance job, packing is often done the day before pickup. Start earlier than you think you need to.
- **Delivery at the other end.** Think through the access situation at your new home. Stairs, limited parking, or rural addresses can all add time and complexity on arrival.

## Choosing a licensed long-distance mover

The biggest risk in a long-distance move is entrusting your belongings to a carrier who either isn't properly licensed or uses a brokered network of subcontractors. Hostage-load situations, where a mover holds your goods pending additional payment, are a real thing and they happen most often in long-distance moves booked through brokers at suspiciously low prices.

Before booking anyone, verify their US DOT number at the FMCSA website. A licensed carrier will provide their DOT number up front and encourage you to check it.
