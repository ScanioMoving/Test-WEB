Walk-ups are part of the fabric of New York. Brownstones in Park Slope, prewar buildings in the East Village, row houses in Astoria: many of the city's most appealing apartments sit four or five flights up with no elevator in sight. Moving into one changes the whole job, and knowing what to expect ahead of time makes a real difference.

## What makes walk-ups different

The obvious challenge is stairs. But a few less obvious things tend to catch people off guard.

**Stairwell geometry.** Prewar buildings often have tight landings and turns that won't accommodate a sofa or a king mattress at full size. The same piece of furniture that slid into a freight elevator might not make it up a curved staircase. Better to know that before move day, not on it.

**No loading dock or service entrance.** In most elevator buildings, movers use a dedicated freight elevator and keep foot traffic out of common areas. Walk-ups typically have one entrance, often a narrow front door, and the truck parks on the street or in whatever spot is available nearby.

**Time and effort.** A walk-up adds time to any job. More trips, more physical work, and more care around walls and banisters on every pass. That's worth factoring into your schedule and budget.

## How to plan ahead

**Walk the route before your move.** If you haven't seen the staircase, go look at it before booking. Note the width, any tight corners, and the ceiling height on the landings. If you can measure the stairwell at its tightest point, share those numbers with your mover so they can flag anything that's going to be a problem.

**Take a hard look at your biggest pieces.** A king bed, a large sectional, a wardrobe: these deserve a second look before the truck shows up. Some items can be partially disassembled. Others may need to go into [storage](/storage) temporarily while you figure out a replacement that actually fits. Better to make that call early than to find out at the top of the stairs.

**Pack smarter for stairs.** Walk-up moves call for smaller, lighter boxes. A 50-pound box of books might manage fine on a dolly across a flat hallway, but it becomes a real problem on a fifth-floor walk-up. Spread weight across more boxes. Your crew will move faster, and your walls and banisters will take less abuse.

**Clear the stairwell ahead of time.** Coordinate with your super and give neighbors a heads-up. Bikes, strollers, and extra furniture stored in stairwells are common in NYC buildings, and clearing the path takes planning, not just a request the morning of.

## A few honest things

Not every piece of furniture will make it up every staircase. If your new apartment has a tight 90-degree turn between the third and fourth floors, some items may simply not fit. A good mover can tell you on a site visit, or often from photos and measurements, whether something is feasible before anything gets loaded onto the truck.

Walk-up moves also cost more than comparable elevator-building moves, because they take longer and require more labor. How much more depends on the number of flights, the size of the move, and how the access looks at both ends.
