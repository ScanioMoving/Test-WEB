Packing fragile items is the part of moving most people underestimate. The boxes look fine sitting in your living room, but a New York City move adds some real challenges: tight stairwells in walk-up buildings, freight elevators with short reservation windows, and a truck navigating stop-and-go city traffic from one neighborhood to the next. Done right, your breakables arrive intact. Done carelessly, they don't.

## Start with the right boxes

Dish packs (also called dish barrels) are the correct choice for your kitchen. They're significantly thicker than standard moving boxes, which matters when something shifts in transit. Don't pack dishes flat. Stand plates vertically, the way they'd sit in a dish rack, with dividers or crumpled paper between each one. Glasses go individually wrapped in packing paper, set upright in cells if you have them.

For everything else, use new or nearly-new boxes. Old boxes that have already been through a move are weakened at the seams and corners. That weakness tends to show up at exactly the wrong moment.

## How to wrap each type of item

**Dishes and ceramics.** Use fresh packing paper, not newspaper (the ink transfers and stains glazed surfaces). Wrap each piece individually, fold the paper over all exposed edges, and tape it closed. Heavier pieces go on the bottom of the box, lighter ones on top. Fill any gaps with crumpled paper so nothing shifts.

**Glassware.** Start with a layer of crumpled paper at the bottom of the box. Wrap each glass from the top down, tucking paper inside the glass before rolling it in more paper outside. Mark the box "GLASS" on all four sides and the top.

**Mirrors and framed art.** These need mirror boxes, which are adjustable flat boxes built for exactly this purpose. Before packing, tape an X across the glass with painter's tape. This won't prevent breakage from a direct impact, but it keeps the glass from scattering if the frame takes a hit. For large or valuable pieces, ask about professional crating.

**Electronics.** Original boxes are best if you've kept them. If not, pack tightly with foam or crumpled paper so the contents don't move at all inside the box.

## The weight rule

Keep fragile boxes under 30 pounds. A heavy box is harder to control on a staircase, and the extra stress on the bottom flaps is real. Label every fragile box on the top and all four sides, in large clear letters. Your crew will orient the boxes correctly and stack them last if the labels are visible from every direction.

## What's different about NYC

Walk-up buildings mean the crew carries boxes up flights of stairs, sometimes through stairwells with sharp turns. Freight elevator windows at co-ops and condos are often just a few hours, which compresses the timeline. City driving also means the truck is stopping and starting far more than it would on a highway run, which puts more stress on the load than a long suburban haul does.

A well-loaded truck accounts for all of this. Fragile boxes go in last so they come out first, and they're never placed under heavier items. If you've handled the weight limits and labeling, your part is done.

## A quick pre-tape checklist

Before you close any fragile box, run through these:

- No empty space inside (fill gaps so nothing shifts)
- Heavy items on the bottom, light items on top
- "FRAGILE" labeled on all four sides and the top
- Bottom seams reinforced with extra tape
- Box weight under 30 pounds

The most common packing mistake is not enough interior cushioning. A box can feel full and still have the contents rattling around once they settle. Every piece needs room to be protected, not just room to fit.
