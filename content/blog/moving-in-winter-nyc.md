New York City in winter is not an obvious time to move. The cold, the short days, the occasional storm, the ice on the stoop. Most people wait until spring or summer, which means moving companies are busier and dates are harder to get. But winter moves are more common than you'd think, and they have genuine advantages, as long as you go in knowing what to plan for.

## The case for a winter move

The biggest one is availability. November through February is a slower period for most movers, so you're more likely to get the exact date you want without booking weeks out. Pricing can be more flexible too, though it varies by company.

There are other practical wins. Street parking is easier in many neighborhoods during winter weekdays, which matters for a moving truck that needs to stay close to your building for a few hours. Building freight elevators and loading docks also tend to be less contested.

## What actually makes winter moves harder

**Weather is the obvious one, but it's not always the problem you'd expect.** Most winter days in New York are cold, not snowy. The bigger risks are ice on steps, loading dock ramps, and sidewalks, and a sudden forecast change that wasn't in the plan. A good mover watches the weather closely and knows when to reach out about adjusting start times or making a contingency call.

**Short daylight hours.** A move that starts at 8 a.m. and runs eight hours in July finishes in daylight. In January, that same move ends in the dark, which affects how crews work and how clearly you can check the state of a truck or stairwell. Starting early matters more in winter.

**Cold weather and your belongings.** This one is genuinely underappreciated:

- **Electronics** shouldn't sit in a cold truck for hours if you can help it. Condensation forms when cold electronics warm up quickly, which can cause damage. Keep laptops, cameras, and instruments with you in the cab or inside as long as possible.
- **Wood furniture** contracts in the cold. Joints can loosen and surfaces can become more fragile. Padding matters more than ever.
- **Plants** will not survive an extended winter move in an unheated truck. Bring them in your own vehicle, or find them a warm home during the transition.
- **Liquids and anything that can freeze.** Cleaning supplies, some cosmetics, and anything water-based should come with you rather than go on the truck.

**Building access logistics.** COIs and freight-elevator reservations work the same way in winter as they do any other time, but buildings are stricter about keeping lobby doors closed. That adds small delays when moving large items in and out. Experienced crews account for this, but it's worth knowing.

## Protecting your floors and walls

Snow and ice get tracked in. A good crew lays floor runners and uses door jamb protectors regardless of season, but in winter this matters especially, because wet boots and slushy dollies can damage hardwood in minutes. When you book a [residential move](/services/residential), ask specifically about floor protection, and confirm it's included.

## Tips for making a winter move go smoothly

- Book a weekday. Even in the slow season, weekends fill up faster.
- Start early. A 7 or 8 a.m. start gives you maximum daylight and means you're mostly done before temperatures drop in the afternoon.
- Check the forecast every day starting a week out. Have a rough backup plan if a storm is coming, even if it's just deciding in advance whether to reschedule or push through.
- Shovel and salt your walk before the crew arrives. It's your responsibility at origin, and a small thing that makes a big difference.
- Keep a clear path inside. Winter coats, boots, and bags accumulate near entrances. Clear those out the day before so the team can move freely from the moment they arrive.
- Keep sensitive items close. Electronics, instruments, and anything that shouldn't freeze should travel with you, not on the truck.

## Long-distance moves in winter

If you're leaving New York or coming from somewhere else, the same principles apply, amplified. Driving conditions on a loaded truck through the mid-Atlantic or New England in winter require more time and more care. For a [long-distance move](/services/long-distance), experienced drivers who know winter road conditions are worth asking about specifically. The timeline should also be a little loose, because a storm that closes I-95 for half a day is real.

## A few things that don't change in winter

The bureaucracy of moving in New York is year-round. COIs, freight-elevator reservations, parking permits in certain neighborhoods, building superintendent coordination.
