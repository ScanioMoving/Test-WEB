New York City buildings run on paperwork, and moving is no exception. If you're relocating into or out of a co-op, condo, or managed rental building, you will almost certainly need to coordinate with the building before the movers arrive. Miss that step, and your crew may show up on move day to find the freight elevator locked and the super turning them away at the door.

Here's a plain walkthrough of what building access coordination actually involves and how to handle it without last-minute scrambling.

## What is a Certificate of Insurance?

A Certificate of Insurance, almost always called a COI, is a one-page document your moving company provides to prove they carry general liability and workers' compensation insurance. It names the building, management company, or condo board as "additionally insured," which means the building has standing to make a claim if the movers damage common areas, the lobby, or the elevator during the move.

Buildings require COIs because they're responsible for shared spaces. If a mover dents the elevator doors or gouges the hallway floor and doesn't have insurance, the building has no recourse. The COI is their protection, not yours.

Most New York residential buildings (and virtually all co-ops and condos) require a COI before any move can proceed. Some buildings specify minimum coverage limits, the name the certificate must list, or additional requirements like an umbrella policy. These requirements vary by building and are usually spelled out in a move-in packet from management.

## How to get the COI sorted

The process is straightforward when you work with a mover who does this regularly:

1. **Get your building's requirements.** Contact your building manager, managing agent, or co-op/condo board and ask what they need. They'll usually give you a form or a brief written list: the coverage minimums, who to name on the certificate, and any deadline.
2. **Pass those requirements to your movers.** A professional moving company will generate the certificate directly from their insurance carrier. You shouldn't have to do anything after handing over the requirements.
3. **Confirm receipt before move day.** Ask the building to confirm they received and approved the COI. Some buildings process paperwork slowly, so give yourself at least a week of lead time, more for larger buildings with formal management companies.

## Freight elevator reservations

Most buildings with elevators have a designated freight elevator (or service entrance) for moving. You cannot use the passenger elevator to carry furniture and boxes. This isn't negotiable, and for good reason: a loaded dolly rolling into a mirrored passenger cab is a liability the building won't accept.

Freight elevator access is usually reserved in advance through the building's managing agent or super. Many buildings allow moves only during specific windows, often weekday business hours or specific morning slots on weekends. Some charge a reservation fee or a deposit.

A few things to confirm:

- The reserved date and time slot, and how long you have.
- Whether the elevator needs to be "padded" (lined with moving blankets) and who provides them. Many buildings require the mover to bring pads.
- Whether there's a building staff person who needs to operate the elevator, or whether the crew can run it themselves.
- Any fee and how it's collected (management vs. the super directly).

## Move-in and move-out fees

Some buildings, especially co-ops and condos, charge a fee or a refundable deposit for moves. It covers the cost of a staff member monitoring the elevator or common areas. These fees go to the building, not the movers, and are usually outlined in your lease or proprietary lease.

## Other things to confirm ahead of time

- **Insurance minimums.** Some buildings require $1 million or more in general liability coverage. Confirm your mover can meet the building's threshold.
- **Certificate timing.** Don't file the COI the day before. Give yourself at least a week for back-and-forth.
- **Parking.** A permitted no-parking zone in front of the building cuts down carry distances. Your movers can advise on the NYC DOT permit process.
- **Commercial buildings.** Office buildings and commercial spaces often have stricter access protocols than residential ones, and may require a building contact on site. See our [commercial moving guide](/services/commercial) for more on that side.
