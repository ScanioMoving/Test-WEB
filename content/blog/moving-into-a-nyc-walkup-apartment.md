Walk-up apartments are everywhere in New York, especially in older neighborhoods across Manhattan, Brooklyn, and Queens. They tend to be charming: prewar details, good bones, real plaster walls. But on move-in day, those same buildings become an obstacle course. Here's what actually makes a walk-up move harder than a standard elevator building, what you can do before the trucks arrive, and what separates a prepared mover from one who isn't ready for it.

## Why walk-ups are genuinely harder

No elevator means every piece of furniture goes up by hand, flight by flight. Sofas have to be tilted and pivoted around landings. Mattresses flex through stairwells that weren't designed with them in mind. Boxes pile up in the lobby faster than the crew can run them, and everyone works harder on every trip.

The buildings themselves add another layer. A lot of NYC walk-ups were constructed between the 1890s and the 1940s. Stairwells are narrow. Landings are tight. Some have low ceilings right at the turn between floors. A few have exterior stairs, which creates its own problem in rain or cold. Every building has its quirks, and those details matter more for a walk-up than almost any other type of move.

Floor count is the other big variable. A second-floor walk-up is an inconvenience. A fifth-floor walk-up is a real physical challenge for the crew, and it shows up in the time and cost.

## What you can do before move day

A little preparation makes a meaningful difference in how the day goes.

**Measure before you commit.** If you can, measure the entry door, the hallway width, and the stairwell landings before you sign a lease on a walk-up. Find out the floor-to-ceiling height on the turns. A sofa that fit into your current apartment may not make it up a prewar staircase.

**Book early.** Walk-up moves take more time than elevator moves, and good movers schedule them accordingly. Try to get on the calendar at least four to six weeks out. If you're moving in peak summer season, even earlier.

**Edit your belongings.** A walk-up gives you a real incentive to sell, donate, or leave behind bulky furniture before the move. Something you might have kept "just in case" is worth reconsidering when it has to go up four flights.

**Clear the path.** On move-in day, the stairwell should be as free as possible. Tell your super or landlord ahead of time so the lobby stays accessible. Let neighbors on your floor know, too, especially if the hallway is narrow.

## What to look for in a mover

Not every company is equally prepared for walk-up work. A few things are worth asking about before you book.

### Crew size

Walk-up moves benefit from a larger crew. More people means more simultaneous carries and less standing around waiting for the stairs to clear. A company that sends two people to a third-floor move is underpreparing.

### Actual NYC building experience

Crews who move New York apartments regularly have seen the full range of stairwells this city contains. They know how to angle a piece through a tight turn, how to protect walls on a narrow landing, and how to pace themselves over hours on stairs. That experience is worth more than strong arms alone.

### Certificate of Insurance

Most NYC buildings require the moving company to provide a Certificate of Insurance (COI) naming the building before anyone sets foot in the lobby. This applies to rentals, co-ops, and condos alike, and it trips up movers who aren't familiar with the city. A professional [residential mover](/services/residential) handles COIs as standard practice. If a company doesn't know what a COI is, that's a sign.

## On pricing

Walk-up moves cost more than elevator moves. The extra time and labor are real. The specific amount depends on the floor count, the volume of your belongings, and the stairwell itself. When getting quotes, tell each company the floor number and something about the stairwell layout. A quote given without that information will likely miss the mark.
